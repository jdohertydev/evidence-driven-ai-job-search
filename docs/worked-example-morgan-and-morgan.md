# Worked example: Morgan & Morgan Business and Technology

This case study applies the workflow to a real vacancy: **AI / Automation Engineer at Morgan & Morgan Business and Technology, Cross Hands, Carmarthenshire**.

The role was surfaced through the wider job-search process because it strongly matched career-fit signals that had already been defined: practical automation, AI-assisted workflows, process improvement, troubleshooting, testing, documentation, implementation and learning unfamiliar technology.

## Two-minute version

I did not start by asking AI to write an application.

I treated the vacancy as an evidence problem:

1. extract what the employer actually requires;
2. research the organisation and separate verified facts from inference;
3. inspect my career and GitHub evidence;
4. map requirements to evidence and expose gaps;
5. choose the strongest defensible positioning;
6. draft and critique the application against explicit criteria;
7. remove or qualify anything I could not defend;
8. keep the final decision with the human.

The result was a targeted application built around demonstrated automation behaviour rather than historic job titles.

## 1. Requirement extraction

The advert emphasised:

- scoping automation requirements and turning business problems into working solutions;
- designing and maintaining automated workflows;
- REST API and webhook integrations;
- automation of repetitive business processes;
- AI-driven solutions using multiple vendors;
- prompt refinement and output testing;
- clear documentation, handover and training;
- investigation of failures and reliability improvement;
- willingness to learn Microsoft and client environments.

A particularly important signal was that the employer did **not** require years of commercial automation experience. It explicitly valued candidates who build things and can demonstrate what they have made.

That changed the application strategy: the strongest evidence was not necessarily the most recent job title. It was the combination of portfolio work, earlier process-improvement examples, technical training and user-facing implementation experience.

## 2. Requirement-to-evidence mapping

Before drafting, I created a simple evidence matrix.

| Requirement | Evidence I could point to | Assessment | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workflow automation | [AI-Assisted Submission-Scope Guidance](https://github.com/jdohertydev/ai-assisted-submission-scope-guidance); [Outlook Class Calendar Automation](https://github.com/jdohertydev/outlook-class-calendar-automation) | Strong | Lead evidence |
| AI-assisted workflows | n8n workflow combining deterministic checks, constrained OpenAI output and human review | Strong | Lead evidence |
| Testing and reliability | Failure-path testing in n8n; dry runs and verification in [PowerShell Photo Archive Automation](https://github.com/jdohertydev/powershell-photo-archive-automation) | Strong | Emphasise |
| Scripting | PowerShell, Python and VBA examples | Strong practical evidence | Emphasise without claiming expert-level depth |
| APIs / webhooks | Portfolio API and webhook examples | Supporting evidence | Use precisely; avoid implying broad commercial experience |
| Documentation / handover | Repository documentation plus historical staff training and user support | Strong | Emphasise |
| Non-technical communication | Technical support, training, implementation and management experience | Strong | Use as a differentiator |
| Power Automate | No meaningful direct hands-on evidence identified | Gap | Do not claim experience |
| Entra / Intune / Copilot Studio | No substantial direct evidence identified | Gap | Present as development areas |

The last two rows matter as much as the strong ones. The matrix is useful only if it makes unsupported claims harder to introduce.

## 4. Positioning decision

My historic job titles do not immediately read as **AI / Automation Engineer**. Trying to disguise that would weaken the application.

The better approach was to change the information hierarchy.

Instead of asking employment chronology to prove I was already an automation engineer, the application led with recent technical and automation evidence. Earlier employment then supported the transferable parts of the role: troubleshooting, implementation, process improvement, user support, documentation and training.

The working narrative was:

> Across different roles and personal projects, I have repeatedly identified inefficient or broken processes, researched solutions and constraints, learned unfamiliar technology, built practical systems, tested and troubleshot them, documented them and helped other people use them. Formal full-stack development training and recent AI/automation projects have developed that pattern further.

This was stronger and more defensible than presenting the career change as a sudden interest in AI.

## 5. Employer research without turning inference into fact

Employer research was used to understand likely context: the company's services, public AI/automation positioning, Microsoft environment, client-facing work and stated values.

I used a simple distinction throughout:

- **verified fact** — directly supported by the vacancy or a company/public source;
- **inference** — a reasonable interpretation that may guide application strategy but should not be stated as established fact;
- **unsupported** — exclude it.

This mattered because employer research can easily become a source of confident-sounding assumptions. The purpose was to improve relevance, not manufacture insider knowledge.

## 6. Drafting as an evaluation loop

The cover letter was not treated as a one-pass generation task.

Drafts were challenged against questions including:

- Does this behave like a cover letter rather than a technical project summary?
- Does the opening explain why this role genuinely interests me?
- Is the strongest evidence appearing early enough?
- Is the language specific or generic recruitment prose?
- Does any sentence imply experience I do not have?
- Are genuine gaps acknowledged without becoming the centre of the application?
- Could I defend every important sentence in an interview?

A polished draft was not automatically accepted. If the structure or emphasis was wrong, it was revised.

## 7. Human / AI responsibility boundary

AI assisted with:

- extracting and comparing information;
- research synthesis;
- evidence mapping;
- identifying possible omissions or inconsistencies;
- drafting alternatives;
- critique and iterative revision.

I retained responsibility for:

- deciding whether the role was genuinely worth pursuing;
- supplying and selecting the evidence base;
- deciding which project claims were fair;
- distinguishing fact from inference;
- deciding which gaps should remain explicit;
- judging whether wording sounded like me;
- accepting or rejecting AI suggestions;
- final submission.

I also used AI during development of some portfolio projects. I do not hide that. The relevant evidence is the problem definition, constraints, implementation decisions, testing, diagnosis, validation and judgement I can explain and defend.

## 8. Final QA rule

Before submission, each important application statement should survive five questions:

1. **Is it true?**
2. **Can I evidence it?**
3. **Is it relevant to this role?**
4. **Is it stronger than the truthful alternatives?**
5. **Could I defend it if challenged?**

If not, revise or remove it.

## Why this example belongs in the portfolio

This project is not intended to replace the technical repositories above. It demonstrates a different capability: how I approach an ambiguous, high-stakes process involving noisy inputs, multiple sources, AI assistance, evidence constraints and human judgement.

The same broad pattern appears in my technical projects: define the real problem, identify constraints, choose an appropriate level of automation, test the result, preserve human control where it matters, document the work and be explicit about limitations.
